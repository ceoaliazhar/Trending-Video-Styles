# Gold Standard: read this before writing anything

Every new script should feel like this one. If yours doesn't, rewrite it until it does.

## Case #1: "Breakfast" (organic, ~100s)
Title / verdict: **The Most Important Meal Was Written by a Cereal Magazine**

> **Mae** *(smoothing Pip's ears; her own plate is empty)*: Eat, Pip. Most important meal of the day.
> **Pip:** I'm not hungry. And you didn't eat.
> **Mae:** I'll eat later.
> **Rennick** *(piling bacon on Pip's plate)*: On the house, friend. Hearty plate, hearty boy. Doctors' orders.
> **Crow** *(two taps on the window)*: Doctors' orders, Rennick? Which doctors?
> **Rennick:** All of 'em. Forty-five hundred.
> **Crow:** Forty-five hundred doctors. Answering a letter from a campaign to sell bacon.
> **Mae:** Crow. Please. Not in front of him.
> **Pip:** Let him, Mom. Let him tell it.
> **Crow:** Shh. Watch.
> *(Drawer 1: an old magazine.)*
> **Crow:** Michigan, 1917. A health magazine prints a line: breakfast is the most important meal of the day. Its editor? A man who co-invented flaked cereal.
> *(Drawer 2: a newspaper.)*
> **Crow:** 1922. A company's bacon sales are slipping. It hires a publicity man. He has a doctor write to five thousand physicians: is a heavy breakfast better than a light one? Forty-five hundred say yes. The papers print it beside "nothing's heartier than bacon and eggs."
> **Rennick:** My father fed this whole town on that plate!
> **Crow:** I know. I painted his sign. *(beat)* "The Most Important Meal." My best line.
> **Crow:** Read the question, Rennick. Heavy or light. Not one word about bacon.
> **Rennick** *(quiet)*: …Pop never read it either.
> **Pip:** So the plate was the ad.
> **Crow:** The most important meal was never a study. It was a magazine, a bacon order, and a crow who needed the work.
> **Mae** *(wraps half the bacon for Pip's bag)*: Eat what you're hungry for, baby. Take the rest for later.
> **Crow** *(to camera, rain stops)*: How many of your habits did someone write just to sell you something? Look again.

## Why it works (6 things, with the principle behind each)
1. **A belief everyone holds as the myth.** "Most important meal of the day" is said at every breakfast table. [P1 gap on a familiar topic, P7]
2. **Stakes in line 1, paid off at the end.** Mae's empty plate → "Take the rest for later." The viewer cares before the facts arrive. [P8 loss, P11 end]
3. **Someone in the room reveals himself.** Rennick can't defend "forty-five hundred"; nobody lectures. [P4 surprise]
4. **The narrator disarms the lecture reflex.** "Shh. Watch." and a confession: the Crow painted the sign. His own skin is in the game. [P6]
5. **Tiny flashbacks:** each drawer = 1 date, 1 plain line, 1 image (magazine, newspaper). [P3 load, P9 concreteness]
6. **The close is the lesson's consequence.** The proof test (the question never mentioned bacon) → verdict = the title → a small repair → a choice. [P6, P11]

For **ad mode**, the same shape ends with the product as the evidence and a short offer line (see `references/script-writing.md`, step 13), never before the verdict.

## The shape (copy it)
Believer + stakes → seller's pitch (the myth) → "Says who?" → seller reveals himself → believer's shock → seller attacks narrator → "He's right. Don't trust me either. Shh. Watch." → flashback (1 date, 1 science line, 1 image) → "Fairy tales!" → proof test → verdict → believer's stake paid off → ritual line + offer.
